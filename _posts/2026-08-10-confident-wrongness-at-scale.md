AI puts powerful methods in the hands of people who couldn't previously use them. Run a complex simulation, build a sophisticated forecast, produce an analysis that used to need a specialist. Connect the tool, ask the question, get a result. On the face of it, that's a genuine benefit. More people can do more.

The risk sits just underneath. The person who understood the method well enough to know when its output was wrong is often no longer the person running it.

## The forecast that arrives clean

Take a forecasting method that runs thousands of simulations across a range of variables. Building one by hand used to force you to understand it. You had to enter the data, which meant seeing when it was inconsistent or incomplete. You had to know roughly what a sensible result looked like, so an absurd one would stand out.

Connect an AI to your data and it does all of that without the understanding. It produces a number. The number looks authoritative. And the person reading it is often several steps removed from the work, with no feel for whether it's plausible, because they never had to develop one.

So you get a leader who wired up the tool, got a figure, and is now telling the team they have ten weeks to deliver something that anyone close to the data would know takes four months. The forecast looks solid. Nobody in the room has the context to challenge it. The firm plans around a number that was wrong before it finished generating.

## Feeling in control isn't the same as being in control

There's a particular trap here. People who craft the prompts feel like they're directing the AI, and therefore feel close to the work. Often they're further from the actual decision than they realise. The work is happening in a black box. The human feels present. The human isn't fully in control.

In a firm where nobody's asking how they'd know if the system was wrong, that gap between feeling in control and being in control is exactly where the expensive mistakes get made. Not through obvious failures. Through confident, plausible answers that nobody's positioned to question.

## What actually protects you

The thing that separates a firm that catches this from one that doesn't isn't the AI. It's whether the human reviewing the output understands the method, the data, and the known limits well enough to open the box rather than trust what came out.

That's not a technical skill. It's judgment, domain knowledge, and a culture where it's safe to challenge an output that leadership has already got excited about. Without those, AI doesn't democratise capability. It democratises confident wrongness, and it does it at a scale and speed that makes the mistakes harder to catch, not easier.

The benefit is real, but it's conditional. It only holds in firms that kept the expertise to know when the clean-looking answer is nonsense. The ones that let that expertise walk out the door on the assumption the tool had replaced it are the ones who'll find out, expensively, that it hadn't.
